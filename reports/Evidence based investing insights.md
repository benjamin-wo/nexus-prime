# Nexus should forecast risk, not returns

Nexus can give its users a real edge, but the evidence says the edge will not come from predicting which US stocks go up. The best machine-learning models explain about **0.4% of a single stock's month-ahead return**, published anomalies lose **about half their premium after publication**, and analyst price targets miss by **45% on average**. Three things are forecastable and well evidenced: risk (volatility, earnings-day jumps, beta, correlation and drawdown ranges), portfolio structure (concentration and where the risk comes from), and the user's own costly behaviour and frictions (overtrading, attention-driven buying, the disposition effect, the 30% US dividend withholding, US estate-tax exposure and FX spreads). The current trade plan needs the most urgent change. In the code, Nexus does not just score a plan the user wrote. It works out the entry, stop and targets itself and labels them with verdicts such as "Buy", "A good price to buy", "Take some profit" and "Time to cut it". It pushes verdict changes to users, and it answers "should I buy AMD here?" with that plan. Its replay odds are drift-neutral by design, which is correct, so they carry no directional information and cannot back up a "Buy" label. In Singapore, the Financial Advisers Act licenses "issuing or promulgating analyses or reports" on investment products, not just personalised advice, and MAS has said a "not financial advice" disclaimer does not cure that. So the verdicts are the riskiest thing Nexus currently ships. The recommended path has four parts: remove the verdicts, make plans genuinely user-authored, rebuild the odds as an honest, calibrated volatility simulation, and spend new effort on risk, cost and behaviour insights. Any true "prediction" feature should come only through a licensed partner.

## Single-stock return forecasts are roughly 99.6% noise

The academic factor literature looks reassuring at first glance. Jensen, Kelly and Pedersen find that **about 85% of 153 factors replicate** across 93 countries ([CBS publisher's version](https://research-api.cbs.dk/ws/portalfiles/portal/95651880/theis_ingerslev_jensen_et_al_is_there_a_replication_crisis_in_finance_publishersversion.pdf); [Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/is-there-a-replication-crisis-in-finance/)). Chen and Zimmermann reproduce 98% of clearly significant published long-short portfolios ([Critical Finance Review](https://cfr.ivo-welch.info/published/papers/chen2021open.pdf)). Replication is not the problem. Magnitude is. McLean and Pontiff's study of 97 predictors finds returns **26% lower out of sample and 58% lower after publication** ([paper copy](https://counterpointfunds.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/PredictabilityMcleanPontiff.pdf)). A larger 199-predictor study finds predictability "decays by 50% post-sample", and theory-motivated "risk" factors decay just as fast as the rest ([Chen, Lopez-Lira & Zimmermann](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2212.10317)). Once microcaps are down-weighted, Hou, Xue and Zhang find **65% of 452 anomalies fail a t>1.96 test and 82% fail the multiple-testing hurdle** of t>2.78 ([RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v33y2020i5p2019-2133..html)). Harvey, Liu and Zhu argue that a third to a half of published factors are probably false discoveries ([NBER w20592](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20592.pdf)). A sensible planning prior is to halve any published premium, then subtract costs.

Costs remove most of what is left. Novy-Marx and Velikov show that only strategies with **monthly one-sided turnover below about 50%** keep significant net returns ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20721)). Momentum's average trading cost of **48.4 bp/month** is nearly nine times value's 5.5 bp ([Alpha Architect](https://alphaarchitect.com/trading-costs-destroy-factor-investing/)). The themes that survive replication, decay and costs are broad, slow, cheap to trade and diversified: value, momentum, profitability/quality, investment and low risk. Even those suffer brutal droughts. Value fell about **55% against growth from 2007 to mid-2020** ([Research Affiliates](https://www.researchaffiliates.com/insights/journal-papers/reports-of-values-death-may-be-greatly-exaggerated)). Momentum has "infrequent and persistent strings of negative returns" that cluster in market rebounds after panics ([Daniel & Moskowitz, NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20439)).

The single-stock arithmetic settles the question for Nexus. Gu, Kelly and Xiu's neural networks, trained on 94 characteristics across about 30,000 stocks, reach a **monthly out-of-sample R² of about 0.33–0.40%** ([NBER w25398](https://www.nber.org/papers/w25398.pdf)). Their impressive Sharpe of about 1.35 comes from value-weighted decile portfolios spanning thousands of names, before costs, so it does not carry over to a 5–10 stock retail portfolio. A large cap with 30% annual volatility moves about 8–9% in a typical month. A strong signal that shifts expected return by 0.3–0.5% a month is about one-twentieth of that noise, which is too small to move a "target before stop" probability in any visible way.

Newer approaches do not escape this. GPT headline sentiment predicts next-day returns in the post-training sample, but Lopez-Lira and Tang report that predictability **declines as LLM adoption rises** ([arXiv](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07619v6)). Separate tests show that memorisation "substantially amplifies apparent predictive power" in LLM backtests ([Gao, Jiang & Yan](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2512.23847v1)). Lookahead "sits in the function rather than the data", so splitting by date does not cleanly remove it ([Lopez-Lira, Tang & Zhu](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.14765v2)). StockTwits sentiment does not predict daily returns ([LSE Business Review](https://blogs.lse.ac.uk/businessreview/?p=22489)). WallStreetBets due diligence was informative before GameStop and lost informativeness afterwards ([RFS via RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v37y2024i5p1409-1459..html)). Retail-facing forecasts are no better. Twelve-month analyst targets imply returns **15 points too optimistic, with 45% mean absolute error, and only 38% are met at the 12-month mark** ([Bradshaw, Brown & Huang](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/reaccs/v18y2013i4d10.1007_s11142-012-9216-5.html)). The notes found no independent out-of-sample evaluation of Zacks Rank or Seeking Alpha Quant ratings, so their track records remain vendor claims. Market timing fares worst of all. Goyal, Welch and Zafirov find that more than a third of post-2008 equity-premium predictors are not significant even in sample, and about half of the rest fail out of sample ([SFI](https://sfi.ch/en/publications/a-comprehensive-2022-look-at-the-empirical-performance-of-equity-premium-prediction)).

| Evidence | What it implies for Nexus |
|---|---|
| ~50% post-publication decay; 65–82% of anomalies fail with microcaps controlled | Show factor characteristics only as descriptive, long-horizon context, never as forecasts |
| Monthly stock-level R² ≈ 0.4% even with ML | No single-stock direction or return prediction will be visibly better than chance |
| LLM backtests inflated by memorisation; social sentiment weak and decaying | Use the LLM for explanation, not prediction |
| Analyst targets +15 pts optimistic, 38% hit rate | Show targets only beside their historical hit rate and dispersion, if at all |
| Equity-premium predictors mostly fail out of sample | No short-term market-regime calls |

Two narrow signals deserve a mention because they are event-based, public and cheap to compute. The first is opportunistic insider buying, which earned about **82 bp/month** in Cohen, Malloy and Pomorski's pre-2012 sample, against roughly zero for routine insider trades ([NBER digest](https://www.nber.org/digest/apr11/w16454.html)). The second is aggregate short interest, which has an annual out-of-sample R² near 13% for the *market*, not individual stocks ([RePEc](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:jfinec:v:121:y:2016:i:1:p:46-65)). Both should be discounted for decay. Neither justifies a "buy" label.

## Volatility, events and correlations are where forecasting genuinely works

Risk is the opposite of expected return: it is strongly forecastable with simple tools. Andersen and Bollerslev show that the low R² critics found for GARCH came from judging forecasts against noisy squared daily returns. Measured against realised variance, standard models are "strikingly accurate" ([EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:ier:iecrev:v:39:y:1998:i:4:p:885-905)). Brownlees, Engle and Kelly find that well-specified GARCH forecasts would have kept even 2008 inside risk managers' **1% confidence bands up to one month ahead**, and that model rankings barely depend on the horizon ([Risk.net](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2161026/practical-guide-volatility-forecasting-through-calm-and-storm)). Among 330 ARCH-type models, plain GARCH(1,1) is hard to beat. For an individual stock (IBM), versions with a leverage effect do beat it ([Hansen & Lunde](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:jae:japmet:v:20:y:2005:i:7:p:873-889)). Corsi's HAR model, which regresses tomorrow's realised volatility on daily, weekly and monthly averages, performs "remarkably" well ([SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1365738)). Option-implied volatility is usually the best single-stock input, but it carries a large variance risk premium, so it overstates realised volatility on average ([CREATES](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/creates/rp/09/rp09_26.pdf)). For a small team on daily data, a defensible stack is an EWMA or GJR-GARCH with fat-tailed errors, with implied volatility added later. Forecasts should be scored with QLIKE, one of only two loss functions whose model rankings stay correct when the volatility proxy is noisy ([Patton 2011](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:eee:econom:v:160:y:2011:i:1:p:246-256)).

Earnings are the largest predictable risk event for a single stock. Option prices separate the earnings-day jump from ordinary daily volatility, and that anticipated uncertainty is "quantitatively large… and informative about future return volatility" ([Dubinsky et al., RFS 2019](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/rfinst/v32y2019i2p646-687..html)). Vendor data puts realised moves at about **85% of the implied move** on average ([ORATS](https://orats.com/blog/marketwatch-talks-to-orats-about-this-earnings-season)). The same vendor reports that 2026 moves have been "consistently larger" than implied, so that discount is not stable ([ORATS 2026](https://orats.com/blog/earnings-straddles-strong-season-2026)). Nexus already records whether earnings fall inside a plan's window, but its odds simulation does not model the jump. That understates the chance of a gap through the stop on exactly the days that matter.

For betas and correlations the lesson is shrinkage and stress. Betas regress toward 1 over time ([RBA summary of Blume](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/1998/1998-02/is-systematic-risk-convergence-a-statistical-artefact.html)). Blume- or Vasicek-shrunk betas beat raw betas on forecast error ([Spoudai](https://spoudai.unipi.gr/index.php/spoudai/article/download/945/1024)). Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage keeps a covariance matrix well behaved with few observations, and it ships in scikit-learn ([sklearn](https://scikit-learn.org/1.4/modules/generated/sklearn.covariance.ledoit_wolf_shrinkage.html)). Correlations **rise in bear markets but not in bull markets** ([Longin & Solnik 2001](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:56:y:2001:i:2:p:649-676)), so diversification fails when it is needed most, and any displayed portfolio risk needs a stressed-correlation view. Tail estimates need humility. A 99% VaR test on 250 days has power of only about **0.13–0.33**, and reasonable power arrives only at around 500 observations ([ECB/Christoffersen](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/pub/conferences/shared/pdf/jcbrconf4/Paper_Christoffersen.pdf)). Nexus should therefore display 95% ranges, which can be checked against data, rather than 99% ranges. Closed-form maximum-drawdown distributions for Brownian motion exist ([Magdon-Ismail et al.](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-applied-probability/article/on-the-maximum-drawdown-of-a-brownian-motion/F9E3B8A454B020DDEBF0AC3390EF7807)). They ignore fat tails and volatility clustering, so they work as a floor, not an estimate.

| Confidence | What to show | How |
|---|---|---|
| High | 1-day, 1-week and 1-month volatility and typical range; shrunk beta; portfolio volatility; options-implied earnings move | Quantile bands from simulation, labelled ranges |
| Medium | 95% VaR/ES; expected and bad-case drawdown over a horizon; correlation and diversification | With a stress scenario and a "can be much worse in a crisis" note |
| Low | Target-before-stop probability; 3-month-plus volatility | As model estimates with drift sensitivity and a published calibration record |
| Not robust | Direction or expected return of a stock; 99%+ single-stock tails | Do not show |

## The biggest measurable edge is the user's own portfolio, behaviour and costs

The strongest evidence-backed "edge" Nexus can offer does not need a market forecast. It needs a mirror. Concentration comes first. From 1926 to 2016, **4.3% of US stocks created all of the market's net wealth above T-bills, and about 58% of stocks failed to beat one-month T-bills over their lifetimes** ([ASU news](https://news.wpcarey.asu.edu/20181212-research-shows-most-stocks-haven%E2%80%99t-outperformed-treasury-bills)). Because long-run returns are this skewed, a five-stock portfolio most likely misses the few big winners. Statman puts the minimum for a diversified random portfolio at 30–40 stocks ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/cup/jfinqa/v22y1987i03p353-363_01.html)), and later simulations suggest far more. Nexus can compute the effective number of holdings (1 divided by the sum of squared weights), each holding's percentage contribution to portfolio risk, and look-through exposure for ETFs. That turns "10 stocks" into "you effectively own 3.4 bets, and NVDA is 15% of your money but 35% of your risk." These are standard calculations. The notes found no study showing that displaying them changes behaviour, so they are evidence-motivated rather than evidence-tested.

Behaviour is where retail investors lose the most. Over the 10 years to 2025, the average dollar in US funds earned **8.7% a year against the funds' 9.9%**. That 1.2-point gap widens to **2.1 points in the most volatile funds**, compared with 0.4 in the least volatile ([Morningstar Mind the Gap 2025](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). Barber and Odean's most active households earned **11.4% a year against the market's 17.9%** with turnover above 250% ([Odean](https://faculty.haas.berkeley.edu/odean/papers/returns/returns.html)). Investors realise winners more readily than losers, even though the winners they sell go on to outperform the losers they keep ([CFA Institute digest of Odean 1998](https://rpc.cfainstitute.org/research/cfa-digest/1999/05/are-investors-reluctant-to-realize-their-losses-digest-summary)). The stocks Robinhood users buy most heavily average **−4.7% abnormal returns over the following 20 days**. App outages reduce that attention trading, which shows the app's own features drive part of it ([Barber, Huang, Odean & Schwarz](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:77:y:2022:i:6:p:3141-3190)). Lottery-stock buyers earn **2–3% a year less** ([Kumar 2009](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jfinan:v:64:y:2009:i:4:p:1889-1933)). Every one of these can be computed from a user's trade log. The candidates are the user's personal behaviour gap (money-weighted against time-weighted return), turnover, a disposition ratio, and a counterfactual showing what would have happened if they had not traded.

The design evidence is clearest about what backfires, and that matters because Nexus ships daily movers, big-move alerts and plan-price pings. In the UK FCA's test app with over 9,000 participants, push notifications **raised trading by 11% and the share of risky trades by 8%** ([Investment Executive](https://www.investmentexecutive.com/news/trading-games-drive-heavier-riskier-activity-fca/)). Field data from a UK broker shows attention triggers increase risk-taking, especially among younger and less experienced investors ([Arnold, Pelster & Subrahmanyam](https://en.wiwi.uni-paderborn.de/fileadmin-wiwi/cetar/TAF_Working_Paper_Series/TAF_WP_055_ArnoldPelsterSubrahmanyam2021_rev.pdf)). In the lab, less frequent feedback leads people to take more equity risk ([Gneezy & Potters 1997](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:112:y:1997:i:2:p:631-645.)). A field-like retirement study did not replicate this ([Beshears et al.](https://nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w16868/revisions/w16868.rev1.pdf)), so the evidence for showing P&L less often is moderate. Pre-committed, automatically rebalanced allocation funds have the narrowest behaviour gaps ([Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/business/insights/research/mind-the-gap-2025)). Vanguard finds no optimal rebalancing rule; annual or semi-annual checks with about 5-point drift thresholds balance risk control against cost ([Vanguard 2010](https://indexacapital.com/bundles/unaiadvisor/docs/papers/2010-Vanguard-Best-practices-for-portfolio-rebalancing.pdf?v=241)). Lump-sum investing beat dollar-cost averaging about **68% of the time** over one-year horizons ([SmartAsset summary of Vanguard](https://smartasset.com/investing/lump-sum-investing-research)). DCA is therefore a regret-management tool, not a return enhancer.

Singapore residents face large, certain frictions that Nexus can quantify. With no US–Singapore income tax treaty, US dividends are withheld at **30%**. Irish-domiciled UCITS funds pay 15% at fund level ([FSMOne](https://secure.fundsupermart.com/fsmone/article/rcms379324/voo-vs-spyl-what-the-etf-domicile-means-for-singapore-investors); [IRS treaty list](https://irs.gov/businesses/international-businesses/united-states-income-tax-treaties-a-to-z)). A nonresident's estate must file once US-situs assets, including US shares and US-domiciled ETFs, exceed **USD 60,000** ([IRS](https://irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/some-nonresidents-with-u-s-assets-must-file-estate-tax-returns)). That threshold is not indexed, and rates above it rise to 40% ([The Tax Adviser](https://www.thetaxadviser.com/issues/2025/apr/estate-tax-considerations-for-non-us-persons-owning-us-real-estate/)). FX conversion costs about **0.15% at Tiger Brokers but 0.5–1% at banks** ([The Kopi Notes](https://thekopinotes.com/articles/etfs/tiger-brokers-fees-singapore-guide/); [SG Finance Calculators](https://sgfinancecalculators.com/invest/currency-conversion-fee-impact-tool/)). Irish funds are "not automatically superior", because expense ratios differ, for example 0.03% for VOO against 0.07% for CSPX ([Saxo SG](https://www.home.saxo/en-sg/content/articles/equities/ucits-etfs-24072026)). Three meters follow directly: a withholding-drag meter (position value × trailing yield × 30%), a US-situs exposure meter measured against USD 60,000, and an FX-cost tracker. These are the most concrete money-saving insights available to this user base. Several points still need a tax professional's sign-off before Nexus states them as fact: whether Singapore levies any tax on capital gains, IRAS's treatment of very active traders, and the estate-tax status of Irish funds. The notes could not confirm them from primary sources.

## Today's trade-plan odds mostly restate the stop-to-target distance

The current odds engine is better than many retail tools. `src/nexus/domain/odds.py` replays the last 252 daily log returns in five-day blocks, 2,000 times, and **centres them on zero drift**, so "a stock that rallied all year isn't assumed to keep rallying." That is the right call. Assumed drift is the main source of model risk in this calculation. Take a +10% target and a −5% stop at 30% volatility, with no time limit. A martingale gives the target a 33% chance of being hit first. An assumed 60% annual drift, typical of a replay on a recent mega-winner, raises that to 56%. That is pure model risk, because drift cannot be reliably forecast. The consequence follows directly. With drift removed, the odds are driven almost entirely by geometry: the distance to the stop relative to the target, cut down by the time window. `src/nexus/domain/plans.py` requires reward-to-risk of at least 2, so a first target sits at least two risk units above entry. With zero drift and no time limit, the baseline chance is then about **one in three** (risk divided by reward plus risk), and the plan's 14-day validity pushes it lower. The odds are honest, but they mostly restate the user's own levels. They say nothing about whether the stock will rise, so they cannot support a "Buy" or "A good price to buy" verdict.

The engine also has fixable biases. It uses the full year's unconditional volatility rather than today's regime, although conditional GARCH or EWMA scaling adapts fast and kept 2008 inside its bands ([Brownlees, Engle & Kelly](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2161026/practical-guide-volatility-forecasting-through-calm-and-storm)). It checks stops only on daily closes. That matches how Nexus scores plans, but a resting stop order fills intraday, and a barrier monitored only at closes needs the Broadie-Glasserman-Kou shift of about exp(0.5826·σ·√Δt) to compare with continuous monitoring ([IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/mathfi/v7y1997i4p325-349.html)). It has no earnings jump. It reports a stop exit at the stop price even though gaps fill through it. Its 2,000 paths drawn from one year of overlapping data are not 2,000 independent observations, so a whole-number "37%" claims far more precision than the data supports. The fix is a filtered historical simulation: de-meaned, volatility-standardised residuals, block-bootstrapped and rescaled by today's volatility forecast, with an explicit earnings jump, daily high/low monitoring, and the expected exit price reported alongside the probability. Optimal block-length selection is available off the shelf ([Patton, Politis & White](https://public.econ.duke.edu/~ap172/Patton_Politis_White_2009.pdf)). The drift stays at zero, or at a modest market-like rate shown as a sensitivity band.

Evaluation has to match. Nexus already logs each plan's first-target chance and compares the average stated chance with the hit rate in `plan_record`. That is calibration-in-the-large, per user, which is a good start but too little data to mean much. The proper standard is to score every displayed probability with strictly proper rules, the Brier score and log loss ([Gneiting & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)), and to "maximise sharpness subject to calibration" ([Gneiting, Balabdaoui & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9868.2007.00587.x)). In practice that means a pooled reliability diagram across all users and tickers, a Brier skill score measured against the geometry-only baseline, and bootstrap intervals clustered by date. Clustering matters because one sell-off hits every stop at once. A skill score near zero means the number adds nothing beyond the user's own levels. That is the honest expected result, and it should be shown rather than hidden. Before launch, the same evaluation should run as a walk-forward backtest that includes delisted tickers, since survivorship can manufacture apparent skill ([Brown et al. 1992](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/5.4.553)). Every parameter variant tried should be logged so the result can be deflated for selection ([Deflated Sharpe Ratio](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551); [Probability of Backtest Overfitting](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2326253)). For modest Sharpe ratios below 0.4, the correct haircut for multiple testing is almost always above 50% ([Harvey & Liu](https://www.cmegroup.com/content/dam/cmegroup/education/files/backtesting.pdf)).

Presentation determines whether an honest number is read honestly. People reason far better with natural frequencies than with single-event probabilities ([Gigerenzer & Hoffrage](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.102.4.684)). Numeric ranges cost only a little trust, while vague verbal hedges cost more ([van der Bles et al., PNAS 2020](https://research.rug.nl/en/publications/the-effects-of-communicating-uncertainty-on-public-trust-in-facts/)). Fan charts convey both expectation and uncertainty and protect the forecaster's credibility when outcomes miss ([Bank of England 2026](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/working-paper/2026/anchors-aweigh-the-effect-of-communicating-forecast-uncertainty)). Spiegelhalter deliberately leaves the outer 10% "not assigned – something else" ([BoE slides](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/events/2025/june/d-spiegelhalter-slides.pdf)). Discrete labels move money regardless of the information behind them. An initial five-star Morningstar rating brings about **53% more than normal flow**, and the star change itself, not the underlying performance, drives the flow ([Del Guercio & Tkac](https://www.atlantafed.org/research/publications/wp/2001/15)). A "Buy" chip on a single stock acts like a star rating. Nexus copy should therefore read like this (numbers illustrative): "In 100 replays at today's volatility, about 30 to 38 reached your target first, 45 to 55 hit your stop, and the rest did neither in 14 days. A plan with these distances and no edge would score about 33. Past patterns, not a forecast; excludes FX, fees and gaps." It should come with an icon array instead of a gauge, and the number should be suppressed when the effective sample is too small.

LLM narration needs the same discipline. With retrieval, GPT-4 Turbo failed **81% of FinanceBench questions** in a vendor test ([Patronus AI](https://prnewswire.com/news-releases/patronus-ai-launches-industry-first-llm-benchmark-for-finance-to-address-hallucinations-301990858.html)), and LLM judges anchor on their inputs even when those contradict the source ([Domyn 2026](https://www.domyn.com/news/finreflectkg-hallubench-graphrag-hallucination-benchmark-for-financial-question-answering-systems)). The safe pattern is compute first, narrate second. Code produces a typed facts payload, the LLM phrases only those facts, and a deterministic checker rejects any number or causal claim that is not in the payload. Nexus's existing `unsupported`/`keep_supported` checks in `plans.py` already follow this pattern, and it should extend to movers and every other narrated insight.

## Data licences bind before data prices do

For a small team, the binding constraint is display rights rather than price. Tiingo's Basic and Power accounts are for personal or internal use, and its documentation says "you may not redistribute the data in any form" without a separate arrangement ([Tiingo docs](https://api.tiingo.com/documentation)). Nexus takes its official closes from Tiingo, according to `CONTEXT.md`, so the account tier and its display rights need confirming in writing now. Financial Modeling Prep requires a Data Display and Licensing Agreement for any display ([FMP](https://site.financialmodelingprep.com/pricing-plans?planType=commercial)). EODHD's commercial tiers start at **$399/month** ([EODHD](https://eodhd.com/commercial-pricing)). A realistic budget is therefore roughly US$200–500 a month for one end-of-day and fundamentals vendor with a written display licence that includes delisted history. On top of that, SEC EDGAR provides free point-in-time fundamentals and Form 4 insider filings, with a 10-requests-per-second fair-access limit and a declared User-Agent ([SEC](https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces)). The Kenneth French library, Open Source Asset Pricing, AQR datasets and FRED are free for research and derived analytics ([French library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html); [OSAP](https://www.openassetpricing.com/); [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Datasets); [FRED](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/docs/api/fred/)). Options data for implied volatility and earnings-move pricing costs about **$199/month delayed** from ORATS ([ORATS](https://orats.com/llms.txt)). It is worth buying only after the volatility and earnings features prove useful. Until then, the stock's own past earnings-day moves, shrunk toward a sector average, are a workable fallback. Licences often treat derived analytics differently from raw quotes, and that should be clarified with each vendor. Scraping unofficial endpoints gives no display rights at all. The notes did not check Finnhub, which supplies Nexus's live quotes, and it should get the same licence review.

## Singapore's FAA catches analyses, not just personalised advice

Singapore draws a stricter line than the US or EU, and the notes reached it through MAS documents and law-firm summaries rather than full statutory text. Every conclusion here therefore needs review by Singapore counsel. MAS lists **"issuing or promulgating analyses or reports concerning any investment product"** as a licensable financial advisory service, alongside advising others ([MAS](https://www.mas.gov.sg/regulation/capital-markets/understand-the-types-of-capital-market-entities/financial-advisers)). Generic content is therefore not a safe harbour. The exemptions are narrow: newspaper permit holders, at most 30 accredited investors, and exempt financial institutions ([MAS FAA FAQs](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/mas/regulations-and-financial-stability/regulations-guidance-and-licensing/financial-advisers/faq/faa_faqs_24nov2017.pdf)). None fits Nexus. MAS has told Parliament that regular recommendations are advice **even without payment**, and that general education is not ([MAS 2025 reply](https://www.mas.gov.sg/news/parliamentary-replies/2025/oral-reply-to-parliamentary-questions-on-financial-services-platform-safeguards-and-complaints)). Its creator guidance states that a "not financial advice" disclaimer "does not absolve you from legal liabilities" ([Yuen Law](https://yuenlaw.com.sg/singapore-law/mas-measures-digital-financial-content/)). Separately, false or misleading statements about capital markets products are an offence under the Securities and Futures Act whether or not the content is advice ([CMS](https://cms.law/en/sgp/legal-updates/singapore-regulator-introduces-conduct-requirements-for-digital-advertising-activities-of-fis-and-warns-finfluencers)). In 2025 MAS said it would send advisory letters to five content creators ([Bird & Bird](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2025/singapore/mas-introduces-initiatives-to-promote-responsible-online-financial-content-and-issues-advisory-lette)). Its enforcement record is light, with six individuals sanctioned for unlicensed advice in three years ([Charltons Quantum](https://charltonsquantum.com/?p=19802)). Light enforcement does not make the activity lawful.

Nexus's current trade plan falls on the wrong side of that line. Nexus computes the entry zone, stop and targets from support and resistance levels. It labels a specific stock "Buy", "Take some profit" or "Time to cut it". With Keep fresh enabled, it re-runs the plan and messages the user when the verdict changes ("now Buy, was Wait"). Its agent skill routes "should I buy AMD here?" and "when should I sell TSLA?" to that plan (`src/nexus/skills/investments/SKILL.md`). That is a regular, product-specific, evaluative output, and it is personalised when the user holds the stock. That is the combination every regime treats as advice or regulated analysis. The "research only" wording does not change this, as MAS has made clear. The probability on its own is much easier to defend. In the US, FINRA Rule 2214 expressly permits "investment analysis tools" that present the likelihood of outcomes, provided methodology, assumptions and limitations are disclosed and results are labelled hypothetical ([FINRA Rule 2214](https://www.finra.org/rules-guidance/rulebooks/finra-rules/2214)). Singapore has no equivalent safe harbour. A neutral simulation of levels the user chose, with method and calibration disclosed and no ranking or verdict, is still the most defensible form available.

The comparison regimes show why US competitors seem bolder. The US publisher's exclusion protects impersonal, regularly circulated content (*Lowe v. SEC*, [Justia](https://supreme.justia.com/cases/federal/us/472/181/)). In 2024 a court extended it to Seeking Alpha's quant ratings, while distinguishing personalised and auto-trading services ([Katten](https://katten.com/insights/advisories/judge-dismisses-case-against-seeking-alpha-implications-for-publishers-of-financial-information)). The SEC withdrew its predictive-data-analytics proposal on 12 June 2025 ([Paul Hastings](https://www.paulhastings.com/insights/client-alerts/sec-withdraws-14-pending-rule-proposals)). The EU splits the question in two. MiFID II licenses personal recommendations, and ESMA says apps can make them ([ESMA35-43-3861](https://www.esma.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2023-07/ESMA35-43-3861_Supervisory_briefing_on_understanding_the_definition_of_advice_under_MiFID_II.pdf)). MAR Article 20 attaches disclosure duties to public ratings and price views ([Skadden](https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2026/10/social-media-meets-financial-regulation)). Nexus's users are in Singapore, so Singapore's rules govern, and personalisation there is an aggravating factor rather than the trigger. A licensed path exists: MAS's digital-advisory exception under FAA-N16 paragraph 11A and Guidelines CMG-G02. It requires a licence, full automation and knock-out suitability questions, and it was scoped around plain ETFs ([MAS response to feedback](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/MAS/News-and-Publications/Consultation-Papers/Response-to-Feedback-on-Consultation-Paper-on-Provision-of-Digital-Advisory-Services.pdf)). Single-stock picking is unlikely to fit even that. MAS's final AI Risk Management Guidelines (7 October 2026, effective October 2027) bind licensed FIs, not Nexus. Their inventory, materiality, testing, monitoring and incident pillars are a sensible voluntary template for the agent ([PwC Singapore](https://www.pwc.com/sg/en/services/risk/straightaways/mas-ai-risk-management-guidelines.html)). Auto-executing plans would bring in dealing and fund-management licensing as well.

If real prediction features remain a business goal, the notes point to three routes. Nexus can supply the technology to a licensed financial adviser or CMS holder that issues the content under its own licence. It can obtain its own FA licence and take on FAA-N16 suitability duties. Or it can restrict such content to accredited investors. Each route is a legal and commercial decision for counsel, not a product toggle.

## Conclusion

The research reframes what an "edge" means for a Singapore retail investor holding US stocks. The edge is not seeing the future sooner than the market, which the evidence says a small app cannot do and a retail trader cannot capture after costs. It is avoiding the losses that are knowable in advance. Those include concentration that misses the few stocks that create all the wealth, trades driven by alerts, the 30% withholding and the uncapped estate-tax exposure, and stops gapped through on earnings day. All of these are measurable, sit on the lower-risk side of the FAA line, and grow more valuable the longer a user stays. That is a better retention story than a buy signal that decays as soon as it is published.

The trade plan is the place to prove this. A drift-neutral, volatility-scaled simulation that openly reports a skill score near zero against the geometry baseline, and a hit rate that matches what it said, will be more trustworthy than a verdict. That record also protects Nexus if a regulator or user ever asks how the number was made. The open questions are mostly legal, not statistical. Nexus needs counsel's view on whether own-portfolio analytics and neutral plan simulations are "analyses" under the FAA, primary-source confirmation of the Singapore tax points, and written display rights from its data vendors. Those answers, more than any model, decide what Nexus can safely ship next.
